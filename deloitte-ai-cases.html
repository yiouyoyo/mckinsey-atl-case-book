Interview Questions
Q1
Framework / Structure
Before looking at the data, how would you structure the question of whether this firm should build a FinOps capability, and what the net value would be?
Model Answer

I'd frame this as a value vs. feasibility vs. risk decision with four MECE branches:

  • Value: How large is the cloud waste problem today? What savings are realistically recoverable through FinOps disciplines — rightsizing, reserved instances, tagging, chargeback? What is the NPV over 3–5 years vs. doing nothing?
  • Feasibility / operating model: What does a FinOps team look like — headcount, tooling, governance? Can this be stood up within the firm's existing cloud COE, or does it require a new function? What is the change management challenge of shifting cost accountability into engineering teams?
  • Cost of the program: Team, cloud cost management tooling (e.g., CloudHealth, Apptio), time-to-savings ramp. Is the ROI positive even in a conservative scenario?
  • Risk of inaction: At what growth rate does cloud spend become existential? What is the strategic cost of slowing innovation if cost pressure forces blunt cuts instead of surgical optimization?

My hypothesis: given the 13× spend growth and zero current governance, the program will be strongly value-positive. The real question is speed of standup and preserving innovation velocity during the change.

Q2
Exhibit Interpretation
Look at Exhibit 1. What does the spend curve tell you about the nature of the problem, and what does it imply about the urgency and sequencing of the FinOps program?
Model Answer

Three observations from the spend curve:

  • Acceleration, not linearity: Spend grew ~$7M in the first two years combined, but ~$15M in 2023–2024 alone. This is compounding behavior — each new product team spins up workloads that are never rightsized or retired. Without intervention, $50M likely becomes $80M+ by 2026.
  • The waste pool is growing with spend: If 32% is waste today, that waste is also compounding. At $50M spend the waste is ~$16M; at $80M it becomes ~$26M. Every year of delay increases the recovery problem.
  • The early years were too small to warrant a formal FinOps function — now they are not: At $4M spend in 2018, a formal team would have been premature. At $50M and accelerating, governance is clearly past the tipping point. The sequencing implication: launch the FinOps team immediately, starting with a tagging and visibility sprint (30 days), then rightsizing and reserved instance quick wins (60–90 days), then chargeback and cultural embedding (6–12 months).

Math / Quantitative
Using Exhibit 2, calculate the Year 1 ROI of the FinOps program. Then determine how large the waste pool would need to be — as a % of spend — to break even on the $1.9M program cost in Year 1. Show your work.
Model Answer — Worked Calculation
Part A — Year 1 ROI: Gross savings potential: $15.4M (37% × $50M, × ~83% recovery rate) Program cost (Yr 1): $1.9M (team + tooling) Net Year 1 savings: $13.5M ROI = Net savings / Program cost = $13.5M / $1.9M = 7.1× (~710% ROI) Payback period: $1.9M / ($13.5M/12) ≈ 1.7 months Part B — Break-even waste % at $50M spend: We need gross recoverable savings ≥ $1.9M to break even. Assume 80% recovery efficiency (not all waste is recoverable instantly). Required gross waste = $1.9M / 0.80 = $2.375M Break-even waste % = $2.375M / $50M = 4.75% Industry benchmarks show cloud waste typically 25–35% for unmanaged environments. The firm would need to have less than 4.75% waste — well below any realistic estimate — to make FinOps uneconomical. Conclusion: The program is robustly value-positive at any plausible waste level. Sensitivity does not change the recommendation.
Q4
Recommendation + Curveball
Give your recommendation to the CFO. Then: the CTO pushes back — "FinOps is just a cost-cutting exercise. If we slow down our engineers with cost approvals, we'll lose our competitive advantage in product speed."
Model Answer

Recommendation: Stand up the FinOps capability immediately. The 3-year net savings of ~$50M at a program cost of ~$4.5M represents an 11× cumulative ROI. Structure the program in three phases: a 30-day visibility sprint (mandatory resource tagging, real-time dashboards), a 60-day quick-win sprint (eliminate idle resources, negotiate reserved instances), and a 6-month cultural embedding (unit economics training for squads, chargeback model, FinOps champion in each product team). Critically, the governance model must shift cost optimization into the delivery cycle — not after it — so engineers own their spend.

Interviewer Pushback

"FinOps is just cost-cutting. Cost approvals will slow our engineers down and kill innovation velocity."

Response: That's the right tension to name, and the Deloitte model specifically addresses it by shifting cost optimization left into the delivery cycle rather than creating a separate approval gate. The goal is not to create friction — it is to give engineers real-time visibility so they self-optimize. A well-designed FinOps model with unit economics dashboards actually accelerates good decisions: engineers stop over-provisioning "just in case" when they see the cost. The firms that implement FinOps with a culture of transparency — not a culture of audit — consistently report that engineers become more deliberate, not slower. The $16M in waste is also capital that could fund two additional product squads.

Interview Questions
Q1
Framework / Structure
How would you structure the build vs. buy vs. combine decision for a B2B commerce platform? What are the key decision criteria?
Model Answer

I'd evaluate this across four MECE criteria:

  • Strategic fit: Is B2B commerce a core competency where the firm needs proprietary control, or a commodity function that a SaaS vendor can execute better? For a heavy equipment OEM, the ordering interface is dealer-facing and often a competitive differentiator — lean toward build or configure-to-own.
  • Functional adequacy: Do existing platforms (A and B) cover critical requirements? If neither does, "keep" options fall away immediately. What gap analysis reveals is the cost of customizing vs. rebuilding from scratch.
  • Economic case: For each option: one-time build/license cost, ongoing opex, time to savings, and NPV over 5 years. Also quantify the cost of delay — every year on the legacy system is a year of waste.
  • Change management & rollout risk: Global rollout to 5,000 dealers across 40+ countries is high-complexity. A phased approach (pilot markets → regional → global) de-risks adoption. Evaluate which option enables phased rollout most cleanly.

My going-in hypothesis: if neither platform is adequate, buying a SaaS solution is often faster but limits customization; building gives control but takes longer. The data will determine which economic profile dominates.

Interview Questions
Q1
Framework / Structure
How would you define and measure success for a public-sector AV mobility pilot serving seniors and people with disabilities? What dimensions matter, and how do you prioritize them?
Model Answer

Success for a public-sector mobility program is multi-dimensional. I'd organize it across four MECE pillars:

  • Access & equity: Are the intended beneficiaries — seniors and people with disabilities — actually being served? Metrics: ridership among target populations, % of trips that replace an inaccessible alternative, geographic coverage vs. need (heat map of rider origins vs. ADA-designated zones).
  • Safety: For AV programs, safety is non-negotiable and binary. Zero safety incidents is the threshold metric, not a goal. Any incident triggers program review. Secondary: near-miss events, emergency interventions per mile.
  • Unit economics: Cost per ride vs. alternative modes (paratransit, rideshare). At scale, the program should approach or beat paratransit's $55/ride. The path to unit economics improvement is utilization (more rides per vehicle-hour) and fleet scale.
  • Funding sustainability: Can the program survive beyond the USDOT grant? Funding mix should shift from 100% grant to a blend of local government, fare recovery (minimal for equity reasons), and potentially Federal transit operating formula funds (FTA Section 5310 for seniors/disabled is a natural fit).

Prioritization: Safety first (program-ending if violated), equity second (it is the mission), unit economics third (determines scalability), funding fourth (enables longevity).

Q2
Math / Quantitative
In the pilot, 3 vehicles covered 1,313 miles serving 100+ riders. Calculate the cost per ride given that AV operating costs are approximately $0.85 per mile (fuel + maintenance) and remote monitoring costs $12 per vehicle-hour. Assume 8 operating hours per day, 90 days of pilot. Does this suggest the program is economically viable vs. paratransit?
Model Answer — Worked Calculation
Step 1 — Variable operating cost (per-mile): 3 vehicles × 1,313 total miles = 1,313 miles (total fleet miles) Variable cost: 1,313 mi × $0.85/mi = $1,116 Step 2 — Remote monitoring cost: 3 vehicles × 8 hrs/day × 90 days × $12/vehicle-hr = 3 × 8 × 90 × $12 = $25,920 Step 3 — Total pilot operating cost (excl. capital): $1,116 + $25,920 = $27,036 Step 4 — Cost per ride: Assume 100 riders, avg 2 rides per rider = 200 rides Cost per ride = $27,036 / 200 = $135 (pilot phase — high fixed cost) Note: At pilot scale, cost-per-ride is high due to low utilization. This is expected — fixed monitoring costs don't scale with rides. Step 5 — Scale projection (10 vehicles, 1,000 rides/month): Variable: 10 vehicles × ~1,500 mi/mo × $0.85 = $12,750/mo Monitoring: 10 × 8 hrs × 30 days × $12 = $28,800/mo Total: $41,550/mo Cost per ride: $41,550 / 1,000 = $41.55 ≈ $42/ride At scale: $42/ride vs. paratransit $55/ride → 24% cheaper ✓ Program becomes cost-competitive at ~800+ rides/month/fleet.

Conclusion: Pilot-phase economics look expensive, but this is a fixed-cost scaling problem, not a structural inefficiency. The program is economically viable at modest scale, and beats paratransit costs at 10+ vehicles with 800+ monthly rides. The USDOT grant is appropriately sized to absorb the fixed costs through the scale-up period.

Q3
Exhibit Interpretation
Looking at Exhibit 2, how does the Accessibili-D AV compare to alternatives, and what does this suggest about who the program should serve and how it should be funded?
Model Answer

Three insights from the competitive comparison:

  • The AV fills a specific gap — on-demand + fully accessible — that no other mode covers: Paratransit is accessible but requires pre-scheduling (often 24–48 hrs); rideshare is on-demand but not reliably accessible; fixed bus is neither. Accessibili-D is the only mode that is both. This is its competitive moat and why the 70%+ repeat rider rate is meaningful — users have no comparable alternative.
  • The $45 vs. $55 paratransit comparison understates the true value: Paratransit delivers pre-scheduled, point-to-point service. Accessibili-D delivers on-demand, flexible, app-based service at lower cost. The quality-adjusted comparison is even more favorable. This strengthens the case for redirecting paratransit funding.
  • Funding implication: The program should position as a paratransit supplement or replacement in covered zones, making it eligible for FTA Section 5310 federal funds (Enhanced Mobility of Seniors and Individuals with Disabilities). This transforms the funding model from one-time grant to recurring federal operating formula — the path to sustainability.
Q4
Recommendation + Curveball
Give your scaling and funding recommendation. Then: the city council member pushes back — "100 riders is not statistically meaningful. How do we know this will work at real scale, and why should we bet public dollars on unproven AV technology?"
Model Answer

Recommendation: Scale the program in two phases. Phase 1 (12 months): Expand fleet from 3 to 10 vehicles, extend coverage to 25 sq miles and 200 stops, targeting 1,000+ rides/month. At this scale, cost per ride falls to ~$42 — below the paratransit benchmark — and the program can apply for FTA Section 5310 recurring funding. Phase 2 (24–36 months): Partner with DDOT (Detroit's transit authority) to formally integrate Accessibili-D into the paratransit network for its coverage zone, enabling rider-facing scheduling to appear in the same app as fixed-route service. Interviewer Pushback

"100 riders is not statistically significant. Why should we bet public money on unproven AV technology at scale?"

Response: That's a fair challenge, and the answer has two parts. First, 100 riders across 1,313 miles with zero safety incidents and 100% satisfaction is not "unproven" — it is proven at pilot scale. The technology works; the question is operational efficiency at scale, which is a fleet management and logistics problem, not a technology risk. Second, the alternative — continuing to fund paratransit at $55/ride indefinitely — is also a bet, and a more expensive one. The right framing for the council is: we are not betting on unproven technology; we are investing $X in a measured scale-up with clearly defined go/no-go metrics at each phase gate. If on-time rate falls below 80% or cost per ride exceeds $55 at Phase 1 exit, we pause. This is how you responsibly scale public-sector innovation.

Q1
Framework / Structure
How would you structure a make-vs.-buy decision for IT operations at a global food company with 100,000 employees? What are the key dimensions, and how do you weight them?
Model Answer

I'd structure this as a strategic fit × economics × execution risk decision with four MECE branches:

  • Strategic fit — is IT a core competency? For a food company, competitive advantage comes from brands, supply chain, and manufacturing — not from running servers. IT operations (service desk, infrastructure, device management) are commodity functions. This shifts the prior toward "buy." Exception: proprietary manufacturing systems or trade-secret IP management might require in-house control.
  • Economic case: Total cost of ownership comparison — fully loaded in-house cost (headcount, benefits, attrition/recruiting, infrastructure, licensing) vs. managed services contract (including transition cost, governance overhead, and SLA penalty economics). Key insight: managed services providers can deliver scale economics (vendor volume discounts, shared NOC/SOC) that an individual company cannot.
  • Capability & quality: Can a partner deliver better outcomes than the current team? For specialized domains (cybersecurity, cloud migration), the answer is often yes — depth of expertise is higher at scale. For food-industry-specific compliance (FDA, HACCP data systems), evaluate partner domain knowledge carefully.
  • Execution & transition risk: What is the transition cost and risk? How long does it take to stabilize? What SLAs guarantee quality, and what are the financial penalties for breach? Who retains institutional knowledge?

Recommendation: Proceed with the managed services engagement structured across five domains (productivity, hybrid infrastructure, identity management, cybersecurity, device management) with Deloitte as primary partner. Structure the contract with: (1) hard SLA minimums with financial penalties for breach, (2) quarterly governance reviews with executive visibility, (3) an explicit knowledge transfer and exit-assistance clause, and (4) retained in-house ownership of manufacturing-critical systems.

Interviewer Pushback

"We're handing our entire technology estate to a third party. What if they underperform, or we want to exit in 3 years?"

Response: This is exactly the right risk to contractually pre-empt, not avoid by not outsourcing. Three mechanisms protect the client: First, SLA financial penalties — if uptime falls below 99%, the contract should specify liquidated damages (e.g., 1% of monthly contract value per 0.1% below threshold). This aligns incentives continuously. Second, an explicit exit clause — the contract must require the vendor to produce runbooks, system documentation, and a 90-day transition plan at any exit point. This prevents lock-in. Third, retained in-house governance — a small internal IT governance team (5–10 FTEs) retains vendor management, SLA oversight, and strategic architecture authority. They are the client's eyes and ears. The companies that struggle with outsourcing relationships are those that hand over governance along with execution. Keep governance in-house, and the exit risk becomes manageable.
